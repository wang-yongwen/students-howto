As you develop your first draft, you should adopt an academic style,
understanding that such a style requires precision but not necessarily
long, polysyllabic words pulled from a thesaurus. Keep in mind the
following:

## Be practical

 - Write what you know and feel, not what you think somebody wants to
   hear

 - Pause now and then to rethink the direction of the paper

 - Write portions of the paper when you are ready; if necessary, leave
   blank spots on the page to remind you that more evidence will be
   required

## Be uninhibited

 - Initial drafts are attempts to get words on the page rather than to
   create a polished document; write without fear or delay.

## Be conscientious about references

 - Cite the names of the sources in your text

 - Enclose quotations in the text

 - Supply page numbers to the sources

## Focusing Your Argument

Drafting the paper will happen more quickly if you have focused on the
central issue(s). Each paragraph will them amplify upon your primary
claim. Do you wish to persuade, inquire, or negotiate? Persuasion means
that you wish to convince the reader that your position is valid and,
perhaps, to ask the reader to take action. Inquiry is an exploratory
approach to a problem in which you examine the issues without the
insistence of persuasion. Negotiation is a search for a solution, the
attempt to resolve a conflicgt by inventing options or a mediated
solution.

## Refining the Thesis Sentence

A thesis statement expresses a theory that you hope to support with your
evidence and arguments. It will perform three tasks:

 1. Set the argument to control and focus the entire paper.

 2. Provide unity and a sense of direction.

 3. Specify to the reader the point of the research.

### Using Questions to Focus the Thesis

If you have trouble focusing on a thesis sentence, ask yourself a few
questions:

 - What is the point of my research?

 - What do I want this paper to do?

 - Can I tell the reader anything new or different?

 - Do I have a solution to the problem?

 - Should I take the minority view of this matter?

 - What exactly is my theory about this subject?

For example, a thesis statement that uses a declarative sentence to
focus the argument toward an investigative issue is as follows:

<example>
Thesis: A delicate balance of medicine, diet, and exercise can control
diabetes mellitus to offer a comfortable life style for millions.
</example>

### Using Key Words to Focus the Thesis

Use important words from your notes and rough outline to refine your
thesis sentence. For example, during your reading of several novels or
short stories by Flannery O'Connor, you might have jotted down certain
issues, such as tragic endings, or human shortcomings. These concrete
ideas might point you toward a general thesis:

<example>
 The tragic endings of Flannery O'Connor's stories depict desperate
 people coming face to face with their own shortcomings.
</example>

### Adjusting or Changing the Thesis

Be willing to abandon your preliminary thesis if research leads you to
new and different issues. For example, one writer began research on
child abuse with the preliminary thesis: "A need for a cure to child
abuse faces society each day." Investigation, however, narrowed her
focus: "Parents who abuse their children should be treated as victims,
not criminals."

## Writing an Academic Title

Although writing a final title may not be feasible until the paper is
written, the preliminary title can provide specific words of
identification to keep you on track. For example, one writer began
with the title "Diabetes." Then she added the phrase "Diabetes
Management." As research developed and she realized the role of
medicine, diet, and exercise for victims, she refined the title even
more: "Diabetes Management: A Delicate Balance of Medicine, Diet, and

## Drafting the Paper

### Writing from Notes

Use your notes and research journal to transfer personal notes, with
modifications, into the draft; transcribe précis notes and paraphrased
materials directly into the text; quote primary sources; and quote
secondary sources. Several suggested methods for building up your
paper from your notes are:

 - Use separate note files within a specially named directory, as
  explained in Unit 9: Writing Notes; use the Insert, Copy, and Read
  commands to transfer your notes into your text.

 - Use two windows on the computer, and begin writing your paper in a
  new file. Find the note you wish to transfer, highlight it, copy it,
  and then paste it into your file.

 - Begin drafting your paper at the top of the file, pushing your notes
  down as you write. When you need a note, find it, copy it, and paste
  it into your text.

 - Begin with the complete outline on file, and enter information onto
  the screen underneath any of the outline headings as you develop
  ideas. You can import your notes to a specific location of the
  outline.

### Writing with Unity and Coherence

Unity refers to exploring one topic in depth to give your writing a
single vision; coherence connects the parts logically by repetition of
key words and sentence structures, the judicious use of pronouns and
synonyms, and the effective placement of transitional words and
phrases. For example:

If we read a book or attend a play, we question the text, we question
the presentation, and we determine for ourselves what it means to us.

### Writing in the Proper Tense

Verb tense often distinguishes a paper in the humanities from one in
the natural and social sciences. MLA style and the footnote style both
require the present tense to cite an author's work, e.g. "Patel
explains" or "Roberts shows." The CMS footnote style also asks for
present tense. Use the past tense in a humanities paper only for
reporting historical events.

### Using the Language of the Discipline

Every discipline and every topic has its own vocabulary. Therefore,
while reading and taking notes, jot down words and phrases relevant to
your research study. For example, a child abuse topic requires the
language of sociology and psychology, using such terms as social
worker, poverty levels, or aggressive behavior. A poetry paper might
require such terms as symbolism, imagery, rhythm, or persona.

### Using Source Material

A writer should weave the sources effectively into a whole; use the
sources as a natural extension of the discussion; cite each source
separately; and provide footnotes in the style appropriate for the
discipline. For more information on incorporating source material into
the paper, see Unit 11, Blending Reference Material into Your Writing.

### Writing in the Third Person

Write your paper with third-person narration that avoids "I believe"
or "It is my opinion." Rather than saying, "I think objectivity on
television is nothing more than an ideal," drop the opening two words
and say, "Objectivity on television is nothing more than an ideal."

## Avoiding Sexist and Biased Language

The best writers exercise caution against words that may stereotype
any person in terms of gender, race, nationality, creed, age, or
disability. Use the following guidelines:

### Age

It is appropriate to use boy and girl for children of high school age
and under. Young man and young woman or male adolescent and female
adolescent can be appropriate, but teenager carries a certain bias.
Avoid elderly as a noun, and use older persons.

### Gender

Gender identifies men and women within their social groups. Use plural
subjects so that nonspecific, plural pronouns are grammatically
correct, such as "Technicians generally maintain their own equipment."
Or reword a sentence so that a pronoun is unnecessary, such as "The
doctor prepared the [not his] necessary surgical equipment." Avoid the
term lady (as in "lady pilot") and use woman or women in most
instances; use female for species and statistics ("10 males and 23
females," "a female chimpanzee"). The first mention of a person
requires the full name (e.g. Ernest Hemingway or Joan Didion) and
thereafter requires only the use of the surname (e.g. Hemingway or
Didion). In general, avoid formal titles (e.g., Dr., Gen., Mrs., Ms.,
Lt., or Professor), and avoid their equivalents in other languages
(e.g. Mme, Dame, Monsieur). Avoid using terms that are not parallel,
such man and wife or seven men and 16 females; instead, use husband
and wife or seven men and 16 women.

### Sexual Orientation

The term sexual orientation is preferred over the term sexual
preference. It is preferable to use the terms lesbians and gay men
rather than homosexuals. The terms heterosexual, homosexual, and
bisexual can be used to describe both the identity and the behavior of
subjects.

### Ethnic and Racial Identity

The terms Negro and Afro-American are now dated and not appropriate.
Use Black and White, not the lowercase black and white. Some persons
prefer the term Black, others prefer African-American, and still
others prefer a person of color. In like manner, some individuals
prefer Hispanic, Latino, Mexican, or Chicano. Use the term Asian or
Asian American rather than Oriental. Native American is a broad term
that includes Samoans, Hawaiians, and American Indians. A good rule is
to use a person's nationality when it is known (Mexican, Korean,
Comanche, or Nigerian).

### Disability

In general, place people first, not their disability. Rather than
disabled person or retarded child say person who has scoliosis or a
child with Down's syndrome. Avoid saying a challenged person or a
special child in favor of a person with      or a child with     .
Remember that a disability is a physical quality while a handicap is a
limitation that might be imposed by nonphysical factors, such as
stairs or poverty or social attitudes.
